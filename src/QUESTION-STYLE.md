# MGT 354: how the professor writes multiple-choice questions

Written from Wesley's classmate's graded Exam 2 (Fall 2026, questions 1–19). **Use this style for every question Claude writes for the game.**

## Format
- 4 options, **a–d**, one correct answer, **1 point each**.
- Stems are full sentences ending in "?" or a colon: "…is called:", "…are known as:".
- Fill-in-the-blank stems use a line: "…leading to applicants receiving a(n) ______ – which often makes them feel underappreciated."
- Negative stems underline the key word: "Which of the following is **not**…"
- Term answers use Title Case (Broad Banding, Forced Distribution system, Referral Bonuses, Market Lead Policy).
- Examples go in parentheses: (e.g., Mercer, Radford, WTW), (e.g., 10–15%), (e.g., 10% top, 70% average, 20% low).

## Two kinds of question, about half each
1. **Applied scenario (long, formal, "HR professional" wording).** Describe what an employer or analyst does, then ask for the concept, the reason, or the right procedure. These often use "According to ___ theory", "What procedure should the analyst apply?" or "Which policy is it following?"
   - *"An employer establishes a total compensation package that features below-market base pay paired with substantial, uncapped performance-based bonuses. According to signaling theory, what message does this structure convey to prospective applicants?"*
   - *"During a market pricing analysis, an HR analyst discovers that an external salary survey job description entails significantly more scope and responsibility than the company's internal benchmark position. What procedure should the analyst apply?"*
2. **Short recall / definition.** One line naming the definition and asking for the term, or the reverse.
   - *"Monetary rewards distributed to existing employees for recommending qualified external candidates who are subsequently hired by the firm are known as:"*
   - *"What is one of the major disadvantages of group incentives?"*

## How the wrong answers are built
- **Absolute or made-up legal language:** "always strictly mandated", "should never overlap under any circumstance", "automatically eliminates", "legally prohibited from exceeding 10%", "Federal law prohibits…", "must adopt the identical…", "Consulting firms are legally required to…". The right answer is almost always the **measured, accurate one**, often the longest.
- **Real terms from other chapters** as distractors (Pay Compression, Collective Bargaining, Due Diligence vs. **Benchmark Conversion**; MBO, Paired Comparison, Graphic Rating Scale vs. **Forced Distribution**).
- **Opposites:** "risk-averse, tenure-oriented" vs. "performance-driven, high risk propensity".
- **"None of the answer choices are correct"** shows up as option d on some questions (and is sometimes the answer).
- Similar-sounding options (Labor Market *Products* vs. *Product Market* Factors).

## What gets tested
- Each big concept shows up **twice**: once as a definition and once as a scenario (bourse vs. quoted-price market: Q1 contrast + Q9 "which scenario"; signaling theory: Q2 + Q16).
- **Current numbers:** "According to national compensation survey benchmarks, what was the approximate average merit increase budget in 2025?" → 3.3%.
- **Guest speakers:** one question (AI → applicants get a "1-second rejection"), marked extra credit.
- Mostly **textbook terms**: benchmark conversion/survey leveling, broad banding, forced distribution, referral bonuses, market lead/match/lag, overlapping pay grades (midpoint differential vs. range differential).

## Answer key spread
The keys ran heavily to **b** (Q1, 2, 5, 8, 9, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19), with some a (3, 4, 12, 13), c (6, 10) and d (11). The game should spread its own answers evenly, but it's worth knowing.

## Checklist for every new question
1. Four options; one clearly best answer, by the class material.
2. Pick the type: an applied scenario with formal HR wording, or a one-line definition.
3. Build at least one distractor from absolute or legal-sounding language and one from a real but wrong term.
4. Underline **not** in negative stems; use (e.g., …) for examples.
5. Where it fits, write the same concept twice, once as a definition and once as a scenario.
6. Cite the source (slide, video, guest speaker, study guide).
